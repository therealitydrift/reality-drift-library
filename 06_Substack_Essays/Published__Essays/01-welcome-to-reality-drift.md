# Reality Drift: Why Modern Life Feels Off

A guide to the cultural forces bending attention, meaning, and time.

“Systems can remain functional long after alignment disappears.”

I was trying to pay a medical bill when the payment link directed me to a third-party portal. Before I could pay, I had to create a new account, verify my email, set a password, and complete two-factor authentication. Then I learned that the specialist’s bill was handled through a different portal, which required another account and another verification process.

When one of the login codes failed, I opened the support chat. An AI assistant asked me to describe the problem, matched a few words in my response, and directed me to an FAQ explaining how to log in. The system had recognized my problem, classified it, and returned the approved answer without helping me solve it.

Nothing was technically broken. The portals loaded. The security systems sent their codes. The chatbot responded immediately. Every component completed its assigned function, yet the simple task of paying for care had become harder to accomplish.

That experience is what first led me toward Reality Drift. Modern systems do not always fail by shutting down. They can remain fully operational while becoming less aligned with the purpose they were built to serve.

## When Nothing Is Broken

We usually recognize failure by breakdown. The website crashes, the shipment never arrives, or the institution stops functioning. But many modern systems fail differently. They continue operating while becoming less responsive to whatever they were built to serve.

A school can report rising test scores even as teachers notice that students are struggling to understand the material. From the system’s perspective, the numbers show improvement. From inside the classroom, something important has deteriorated. The problem is that the measurement no longer provides a reliable account of whether the purpose beneath it is being served.

Reality Drift is that condition. It is the gradual process through which a system remains coherent and operational while losing alignment with the reality it was meant to represent, govern, or support.

## How Drift Begins

Complex systems cannot take in reality whole, so they run on simplified stand-ins. A company uses performance metrics to represent work, while a platform uses clicks and viewing time to estimate relevance. These representations are necessary because coordination at scale would be impossible without them. Drift begins when the stand-in becomes easier to manage than the reality behind it.

A metric is easy to place on a dashboard and tie to rewards. The quality of the work beneath it is much harder to capture. As the metric gains authority, people begin shaping their activity around what the dashboard can recognize. Eventually, improving the indicator becomes more important than improving the work. The representation is no longer reporting on the system. It is reorganizing the behavior inside it.

## Why Systems Do Not Correct

A drifting system can still receive accurate feedback. The problem may be widely reported and clearly visible in the data. The system may know exactly where people are getting stuck. What has weakened is the ability of that detection to force a change.

This is different from Goodhart’s law. Goodhart describes a proxy becoming unreliable because people begin optimizing for it. A performance measure becomes a target, behavior bends around the target, and the measure stops reflecting the reality it was meant to capture.

Reality Drift describes a broader failure. The proxy may be distorted, but it does not have to be. A billing system might accurately record that thousands of patients cannot access the payment portal. The complaints are real, the data is correct, and the failure is visible. Yet the system can process every complaint without changing the structure causing it.

The evidence becomes another administrative object. The complaint becomes a ticket, the ticket is categorized, and the completed response counts as resolution even when the original problem remains. Feedback is present and may even be accurate. It simply no longer binds the system to correction.

## What Drift Feels Like

From inside, drift rarely feels like collapse. It feels like friction. You spend more time working the process than addressing the need. You hand over information the system already has. You complete every step without getting closer to a resolution, while the official description of what you are doing remains accurate in a narrow sense and increasingly detached from the experience itself. The language grows more polished as the outcomes become harder to verify.

That friction usually lands on the individual. At work, someone may spend hours reconciling contradictory priorities, moving information between systems, and documenting progress for people who can already see the same dashboard. The organization appears efficient because the labor required to hold its fragmented processes together is absorbed by employees and users. Its complexity becomes their cognitive burden.

## When Optimization Changes the Purpose

Drift often accelerates through optimization. A platform needs some basis for deciding what to show, but the trap begins when improving that measurable objective starts displacing the purpose it was meant to support. The system becomes steadily better at holding attention, even though attention was never the same as value.

Once a proxy is embedded in incentives, interfaces, and decisions, people begin adapting to it. The behavior produced by that adaptation generates new data, which the system interprets as evidence that the proxy was right. A recommendation shapes what people watch, and their viewing behavior is then treated as an independent record of what they wanted. The system measures behavior it helped produce and uses the result to reinforce the representation that shaped it.

## Recognizing Reality Drift

Reality Drift names the gap that opens when a system remains operational while becoming less constrained by the reality it was built to serve. It may continue producing information, completing procedures, and responding to feedback. The question is whether any of that can still force a meaningful correction.

This is why modern life can feel wrong when nothing appears broken. The official account remains orderly, but no longer matches the experience of moving through the system. Complexity gets absorbed by individuals, failures become administrative objects, and continued operation is mistaken for evidence of alignment.

Recognizing drift means looking past whether the procedure was followed or the target was reached. Is the underlying purpose still being served?

Reality Drift begins from one premise: The absence of failure is not proof of alignment. A system can keep working long after its connection to reality begins to weaken.
