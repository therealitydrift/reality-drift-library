# Constraint Collapse: Terrence Deacon and the Structure of Meaning

Why modern life feels unstable when information outruns the constraints that give experience shape.

*“Fluency is what systems sound like after constraint collapses.”*

Modern systems are remarkably good at producing information, and they now produce it faster than anyone can read it. The expansion has not produced a corresponding increase in meaning, and in many settings the opposite appears to be happening. Information grows while orientation weakens, and systems become more articulate while the people using them become less certain what the outputs mean.

Terrence Deacon offers a way into this. In *Incomplete Nature*, he asks how purpose and meaning arise inside a physical universe, and his answer does not begin with information as something added to matter. It begins with constraint, with the exclusion of possible states rather than the presence of material ones. Water runs down a channel because the channel excludes other paths. At higher levels of organization, meaning also depends partly on excluded possibilities: a sign signifies through the alternatives and interpretations its context rules out.

**Author’s Note:** *Deacon’s account is not a theory of modern institutions or technological systems, and constraint collapse is not his term. What follows extends his underlying insight. If organized processes depend on constraints that make some outcomes consequential rather than others, then a system can remain operational while losing the particular constraints that once forced it to correct course.*

## What Constraint Makes Possible

A constraint on its own only narrows what can happen, and this is as true of information as it is of water. We tend to assume otherwise, that more data will produce a better model and more documentation will produce more clarity. Sometimes it works out that way, since information can reduce uncertainty and expose patterns that would otherwise stay invisible. But a test result means one thing in a healthy forty-year-old and something else entirely in someone with a family history, and the number itself does not contain that difference. What supplies it is the wider context that makes some interpretations relevant and rules out others. Information only becomes useful inside a structure that distinguishes what matters from what does not, and that structure is built out of exclusions rather than additions.

Strip those away and the information stays technically intact while becoming difficult to interpret, which is why systems can hold enormous quantities of accurate information and still fail to orient the people using them.

Something more is needed before a system can be wrong. In Deacon’s account, error becomes possible only within an organized process that tends toward maintaining certain states rather than others, so that some outcomes sustain it and others threaten it. A rock cannot be wrong about anything. A cell can, because its entire organization is arranged around staying within the set of states that keep it alive.

That asymmetry is where error comes from. Failure requires something being held, and correction requires that failing to hold it costs something.

## Constraint Collapse

Constraint collapse is the condition in which feedback remains present and stops binding. This is not the absence of feedback. Errors get detected, postmortems get written, and dashboards flag the anomaly. What has gone is the connection between detecting the error and being made to do anything about it. The system stays informed and stops being accountable, and those two things turn out to be separable in a way most accounts of institutional failure assume they are not.

This is also different from Goodhart’s law. Goodhart describes what happens when a proxy becomes a target and begins to lose its relationship to the condition it represents. Constraint collapse names a broader failure: the system has lost the capacity to let any measure invalidate its course. The problem is no longer only what is being measured. It is whether measurement can still stop anything.

Complex systems drift when the constraints that preserve operation become stronger than those that preserve correspondence with reality. Feedback continues to circulate, but its consequences are absorbed, displaced, or converted into procedure. The system remains organized while losing the conditions under which error can correct it.

## Why It Looks Like Order

Drifting systems do not lose their rules. They gain rules, because every visible failure produces a new form and a new sign-off. Each addition records that the problem was taken seriously rather than constraining whether it happens again. The number of rules rises while their binding force falls. From inside, the two are easy to confuse, since the growing apparatus is the most visible evidence the organization has that it is responding to anything at all.

Correction requires that failing to hold the state costs something, and it costs less every time feedback arrives late or lands on someone other than whoever made the decision. Eventually hitting the metric is easier than doing the thing the metric stood for, and once that is true the metric is what gets done. The paperwork says the action was completed, the condition it was supposed to change is still sitting there, and nothing in the system is built to notice the difference.

This is why drift looks like order rather than chaos. Dashboards stay green and process gets tighter while the apparatus loses any way of telling whether it is working. A hospital can satisfy every documentation requirement it has while nobody inside it can tell a patient what is happening to them, and nothing in the documentation registers that as a failure, because the documentation was never what the hospital was for. The constraints hold. They stopped being attached to the outcome.

## How Constraints Get Substituted

Systems rarely operate without constraints. They replace difficult constraints with manageable ones, so that contact with patient outcomes becomes compliance with documentation and learning becomes performance on an assessment. The substitute is not arbitrary and usually begins as a practical representation of something real. The problem emerges as the system becomes more responsive to the representation and less responsive to the condition it was built to track, until success against the proxy protects it from correction by the outcome.

This is where the condition becomes difficult to reverse. The substitute constraint is doing real work, holding coordination together and making the organization legible to itself, so removing it looks like removing the only thing keeping order. The system ends up defending the mechanism that severed it from the outcome, and the usual response to visible drift, which is more monitoring and more reporting, adds another layer of representation where a binding constraint was needed.

## When Fluency Outlives Contact

Language models make the condition legible. A model’s outputs are shaped by its training data, architecture, objectives, and the statistical regularities learned from language. But coherence inside a representational system is not correction by the world being represented. A model can summarize a document while flattening the one distinction the reader needed, or satisfy the form of an answer long after the evidential basis for that answer has weakened. The output stays fluent because linguistic coherence is one of the constraints it was produced under. What is missing is a constraint strong enough to tie that fluency to the specific reality outside the model.

Even when corrections are incorporated into later tuning or evaluation, they enter as additional representational signals. They may improve the form of later outputs without restoring contact with the particular reality that prompted the correction. This is what semantic fidelity asks about: whether meaning survives when a representation stays readable and internally consistent after losing the context that made it worth reading. Fluency can persist after much of that context has been lost.

## Continuation Without Correction

When constraints stop binding, criticism becomes something a system can absorb. It answers by producing more language and more evidence of its own compliance, and because its operational constraints are intact, it continues. Because its corrective constraints are not, continuing no longer demonstrates that anything is aligned.

This is survivable wrongness, where a system stays coherent enough to persist while becoming steadily less answerable to the reality it represents. Deacon’s contribution is to show that absence is not empty, and that what gets prevented organizes what actually happens. Reality drift begins when systems preserve the constraints required for continuation and lose the constraints required for correction.
